Start here with a pen, paper, and an ordinary calculator. Read **one lesson per session**, taking 5–10 minutes to read, work the example, answer, and explain the idea aloud. You may stop after one lesson. ABC and every price below are fictional. No brokerage account, coding, or real money is needed. Stock examples ignore dividends, fees, and taxes unless stated.

## Lesson 01: your first goal is understanding

**Time: 5–10 minutes. One idea:** learning a trading skill and making money are different outcomes.

An investment can lose value. For now your job is to understand a decision before making it. Completing this course cannot promise a profit. Your first measurable goal is: “I can explain an example and solve a changed version.” Keep any money needed for everyday life outside these exercises. The [SEC explains investment risk](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/what-risk).

**Worked example:** Mira uses $100 of imaginary money in a notebook. Her first answer is wrong; she fixes it and solves a new example. She has learned something. Her actual bank balance has changed by $0.

**Try it:** Dev doubles an imaginary $50 by guessing. Can that one result establish that Dev understands trading? Explain in one sentence.

<details>
<summary>Answer — open after your attempt</summary>

No. Imaginary $50 becoming $100 records one outcome; it does not show an understood or repeatable decision process. Ask Dev to explain the risk and solve an unfamiliar example.

</details>

## Lesson 02: a share is a piece of ownership

**Time: 5–10 minutes. One idea:** a stock share represents ownership in a company.

A company is a business. A **share** is one unit of its stock. The share's market price can rise or fall; ownership is not a promise that you will get your purchase money back. Our imaginary business is called ABC. [SEC: stocks](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/stocks).

**Worked example:** Imagine ABC has exactly 100 equal shares and you own 2. Your slice is `2 ÷ 100 = 0.02`, or 2% of those shares. This tiny made-up company makes the idea visible; real public companies often have far more shares.

**Try it:** A fictional business has 200 equal shares. You own 10. What percentage of those shares do you own?

<details>
<summary>Answer — open after your attempt</summary>

`10 ÷ 200 = 0.05 = 5%`. You own 5% of the shares in this simplified example. This does not tell you what their future price will be.

</details>

## Lesson 03: turn a share price into total dollars

**Time: 5–10 minutes. One idea:** price per share and total position value are different quantities.

Multiply the number of shares by the dollars for each share. Write the units: `shares × dollars per share = dollars`. A **position** just means an amount of an investment you hold. This lesson uses arithmetic, not a forecast.

**Worked example:** You buy 3 ABC shares for $20 each. Cost is `3 × $20 = $60`. If each is later worth $18, the position is worth `3 × $18 = $54`. The change is `$54 − $60 = −$6`, a $6 loss before costs.

**Try it:** You buy 4 shares at $15 each. They later reach $17 each. Find the starting cost, new value, and dollar gain before costs.

<details>
<summary>Answer — open after your attempt</summary>

Starting cost: `4 × $15 = $60`. New value: `4 × $17 = $68`. Gain: `$68 − $60 = $8`. The quoted price rose by $2 per share, so four shares gained $8.

</details>

## Lesson 04: a percentage needs a starting amount

**Time: 5–10 minutes. One idea:** percentage return compares a gain or loss with what you started with.

Use `return = (ending value − starting value) ÷ starting value × 100%`. Here, “return” means the percentage change in value, ignoring costs and other cash flows. The denominator—the number below the division sign—is the starting value.

**Worked example:** $80 becomes $88. Gain is `$88 − $80 = $8`. Return is `$8 ÷ $80 = 0.10 = 10%`. If $80 instead becomes $72, return is `−$8 ÷ $80 = −10%`.

**Try it:** A hypothetical position falls from $50 to $40. What percentage did it lose? What percentage rise from $40 would restore $50?

<details>
<summary>Answer — open after your attempt</summary>

Loss: `($40 − $50) ÷ $50 = −20%`. Recovery: `($50 − $40) ÷ $40 = 25%`. The dollar change is $10 both ways, but the starting amount changes. A 20% fall and 20% rise do not cancel.

</details>

## Lesson 05: an ETF share and an index are different things

**Time: 5–10 minutes. One idea:** a fund can have shares you buy, while an index is a calculated measure.

An **exchange-traded fund (ETF)** pools investments and has shares that trade on an exchange. An **index** measures a chosen group using a calculation. An ETF may aim to track an index, but the index itself is not a share. [SEC: ETF](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/exchange-traded-fund-etf), [SEC: market index](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/market-index).

**Worked example:** Our fictional ABC Index displays 1,000 points. A separate fictional fund, ABC Basket ETF, trades at $25 per share. Buying 2 ETF shares costs `2 × $25 = $50` before fees. You bought fund shares; you did not buy two units of the index's 1,000-point reading.

**Try it:** A fictional index reads 2,500 points, while a fund following it trades at $40 a share. What does buying 3 fund shares cost before fees, and what do you own?

<details>
<summary>Answer — open after your attempt</summary>

`3 × $40 = $120`. You own three shares of the fund. You do not own three shares of the calculated index, and the index level is not the ETF's share price.

</details>

## Lesson 06: your broker connects your order to markets

**Time: 5–10 minutes. One idea:** your account provider and a trading venue have different jobs.

A **broker** handles your customer account and orders. An **exchange** is a trading venue with rules for matching trading interest. Seeing a button in a broker's app does not mean a trade has happened; an order must actually receive a **fill**, meaning an execution. [SEC: market participants](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/how-stock-markets-work/market-participants).

**Worked example:** Leena enters a practice instruction to buy 1 ABC share. Her notebook shows “submitted” and zero shares. Then the simulated order fills at $30. Only after that fill does her notebook show 1 share and a $30 purchase, before fees.

**Try it:** A practice broker screen shows an order to buy 2 shares as “open, unfilled.” How many shares did that order buy so far?

<details>
<summary>Answer — open after your attempt</summary>

Zero. An unfilled instruction is not a completed purchase. The requested quantity is 2; the executed quantity is 0.

</details>

## Lesson 07: buying and selling have different displayed prices

**Time: 5–10 minutes. One idea:** the bid–ask spread creates a trading cost.

The **bid** is a displayed buying price from the market; the **ask** is a displayed selling price. You might buy at the ask and sell at the bid. Their difference is the **spread**. Displayed prices and available quantities can change before an order fills. [SEC: bid and ask](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/ask-price).

**Worked example:** One ABC share is quoted $19 bid / $20 ask. Buy one at $20, then immediately sell at the unchanged $19 bid. Result: `$19 − $20 = −$1` before fees. The business did not need to change for that loss to happen.

**Try it:** A share shows $29.50 bid / $30 ask. What is the loss from buying 2 at the ask and immediately selling 2 at the unchanged bid, before fees?

<details>
<summary>Answer — open after your attempt</summary>

Buy cost: `2 × $30 = $60`. Sale proceeds: `2 × $29.50 = $59`. Loss: `$59 − $60 = −$1`, or $0.50 for each share. This assumes both displayed prices have enough size and remain available.

</details>

## Lesson 08: a limit controls price, not whether you trade

**Time: 5–10 minutes. One idea:** a limit order states your worst acceptable execution price.

A buy limit sets the highest price you permit; a sell limit sets the lowest. The order may never fill. A **market order** asks for prompt execution at available prices without the same price limit. [SEC: types of orders](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/how-stock-markets-work/types-orders).

**Worked example:** ABC's ask is $10. You write a practice buy limit of $9.80 for one share. If a seller accepts $9.80, it can fill there. If no one sells at $9.80 or lower while your order is active, you buy nothing. Your limit does not turn the ask into $9.80.

**Try it:** Your practice sell limit is $12 per share. Can a normal fill occur at $12.20? At $11.90? Is any fill guaranteed?

<details>
<summary>Answer — open after your attempt</summary>

$12.20 is permitted because it is above your $12 minimum. $11.90 is below that minimum and is not permitted by the limit. A fill is not guaranteed.

</details>

## Checkpoint 1: try five new questions

Close the lessons and show your reasoning. All numerical examples ignore fees and dividends.

1. You buy 5 ABC shares for $12 each. Their price reaches $13. Find cost, new value, dollar gain, and percentage return.
2. Does a reading of 3,000 on an index mean you own a share worth $3,000? Explain.
3. A practice share quote is $7.80 bid / $8 ask. Find the unchanged ask-to-bid loss for 3 shares.
4. **Safety:** A buy limit is $7.90 while the ask is $8. Are you guaranteed to own shares at $7.90? What maximum price does the limit authorize before fees?
5. **Safety:** An order is submitted but unfilled. Did you buy anything, and does passing this checkpoint establish that you can make money trading?

<details>
<summary>Checkpoint 1 worked key — open after all five attempts</summary>

1. Cost `5 × $12 = $60`; value `5 × $13 = $65`; gain `$5`; return `$5 ÷ $60 × 100% = 8⅓%`.
2. No. The index is a calculated reference level. Owning fund or company shares is a different thing.
3. Cost `3 × $8 = $24`; proceeds `3 × $7.80 = $23.40`; loss `$0.60`.
4. No fill is guaranteed. If the buy limit fills, the purchase price is at most $7.90 per share, excluding fees.
5. No shares were bought by that unfilled order. Passing demonstrates these concepts, not profitable trading.

</details>

**Pass rule:** answer all five correctly and explain both safety items without notes. For an error, reread only that lesson, explain the missed step, and ask your tutor for a changed-number version. Save the new attempt before checking it; repeat until every item is correct. Move on at your pace.
