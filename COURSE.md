# Optional advanced track: the 104-week options curriculum

**Start with the [beginner microcourse](beginner/README.md) at 10–15 minutes per study day.** This longer map is an optional reference and advanced study path. Its quantitative programming, pricing, and research modules are not prerequisites for beginning the microcourse, understanding options, choosing not to trade, or considering personal suitability. Completing either path does not approve anyone for live trading.

**Audience:** learners choosing deeper study, with an early Python path for those who already code. **Market:** U.S. listed options. **Optional pace:** 10 hours per week for 104 weeks (about 1,040 hours). **Outcome:** a core within this advanced path in contracts, risk, execution, mathematics, and research, followed by [personal portfolio, independent research, or professional quant paths](MASTERY_TRACKS.md). The timeline extends when prerequisites or gates need more work. World-class performance cannot be promised by a syllabus and would require continued evidence over years and market regimes.

The sequence is intentionally cumulative. A person who knows many strategy names but cannot explain assignment, identify bad data, or measure costs is not ready to take risk.

## How to study each week

| Activity | Hours | Evidence to keep |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Read or watch one primary lesson | 2 | Notes in your own words; three questions |
| Solve problems or build a small tool | 3 | Worked answers, code, or diagrams |
| Observe real option chains and product specifications | 2 | Dated observations; no need to trade |
| Write a journal or research memo | 2 | Decision, uncertainty, what would change your mind |
| Recall quiz and review errors | 1 | Corrected mistakes and next action |

Once simulation begins, replace some observation time with carefully selected paper decisions. A simulator is a teaching tool; its fills can differ from live fills. [IBKR documents several paper trading limitations](https://www.ibkrguides.com/orgportal/aboutpapertradingaccount.htm).

**Advancement rule:** Complete the sprint artifact and meet its pass standard before starting the next sprint. For ordinary concepts, aim for at least 90% on a closed-note recall quiz and correct any misses. For contract size, maximum loss, exercise, assignment, settlement, and margin questions, require 100% accuracy in fresh scenarios. Rework mistakes; time spent repeating a sprint is productive. The eight-week paper-decision record is the one planned overlap: its later weeks may run alongside Modules 13–14, but Risk Gate 2 remains open until every requirement is met.

Begin with the diagnostic in [the foundation math bridge](FOUNDATION_MATH_BRIDGE.md). Keep original answers, corrections, later fresh retests, code versions, and reviewer decisions in [the learning record](LEARNING_RECORD_TEMPLATE.md). If you already know Python, demonstrate it with a small clean-data and hand-check exercise, then use programming for early payoff and data labs. You may replace Module 15's introductory syntax work with a more demanding artifact, but its reproducibility gate still applies. No diagnostic bypasses the contract or safety exams.

## Stage 1 — financial and options literacy (weeks 1–24)

The full explanations, worked examples, weekly plans, and answer keys are in [Teaching Volume 1](LESSONS_01_FOUNDATIONS.md).

Read the opening lesson in [Volume 1, Module 1](LESSONS_01_FOUNDATIONS.md), then complete [the seven-day introduction](ADVANCED_FIRST_WEEK.md). Practice percentages, units, and break-even algebra with the [math bridge](FOUNDATION_MATH_BRIDGE.md). Use [the separate foundation assessment bank](FOUNDATION_ASSESSMENTS.md) and [answer key](FOUNDATION_ANSWER_KEY.md) for fresh cases. Use the free [SEC introduction](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins-63), [OIC Options Academy](https://prd-web.optionseducation.org/theoptionseducationcenter/occ-learning), and [Cboe Options Institute](https://www.cboe.com/optionsinstitute/learningportal/). Read the current [OCC options disclosure document](https://www.theocc.com/company-information/documents-and-archives/options-disclosure-document) before any simulation of a strategy and again before any live trading.

**Gate A:** Explain an option quote, draw the four basic payoffs, distinguish a right from an obligation, compute contract dollar amounts, and handle assignment/settlement cases without help. Maintain at least 40 accurately explained terms accumulated across Stage 1. No live trades.

## Stage 2 — how option prices and trades behave (weeks 25–48)

Study [Teaching Volume 2](LESSONS_02_BEHAVIOR.md) alongside this map.

Use [OIC Greeks and volatility modules](https://prd-web.optionseducation.org/theoptionseducationcenter/occ-learning), [IBKR's recommended options course sequence](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/campus/traders-academy/finance-courses/), and [OIC's delayed calculators](https://www.optionseducation.org/Options-Quotes-Calculators). A calculator checks work; it does not replace explanation.

| Sprint | Weeks | Learn and practice | Required artifact and pass standard |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7. Greeks I | 25–28 | Delta, gamma, theta, vega, rho; local approximations and why they break on large moves. | Predict the direction of price changes in 30 stock/IV/time/rate scenarios, then compare with a calculator and explain errors. |
| 8. Volatility I | 29–32 | Realized versus implied volatility, annualization, event volatility, skew, term structure; IV rank versus percentile. | A weekly volatility diary for two underlyings. State what each measure says and what it does *not* prove about value or future profit. |
| 9. Greek combinations | 33–36 | Net position Greeks, nonlinear exposure, volatility crush, calendars and term structure, delta hedging concept, correlation risk. | Five scenario matrices for multi-leg positions over changes in price, IV, and days to expiry, plus the [discrete hedge ledger](DYNAMIC_HEDGING_LAB.md). Include a calendar, which still has a live long leg when its short leg expires; reconcile the hedge cash and residual exposure. |
| 10. Trade design | 37–40 | Thesis, time horizon, alternatives, target exposure, rejection criteria, event calendar. | Write 10 hypothetical trade tickets and 10 explicit no-trade decisions. Each ticket must say why stock or cash is not a better expression. |
| 11. Execution | 41–44 | NBBO, spread, depth, limit orders, complex orders, fees, partial fills, slippage, exit liquidity. | Observe 30 option quote changes and complete the [timestamped execution replay](EXECUTION_PRACTICE.md). Record order intent, nonfills, cancellations, conservative entry/exit estimates, and uncertainty about queue position; do not assume midpoint fills. |
| 12. Simulation and review | 45–48 | Simulator setup, written pretrade plan, journal, error taxonomy, weekly review, benchmark. | Begin the eight-week paper-decision record specified by [Risk Gate 2](RISK_AND_OPERATIONS.md). Log every eligible setup, pass, failed order, costs, drawdown, and rule violation. Continue the record alongside Modules 13–14 until eight weeks and the gate conditions are met; no forced trade count. |

**Gate B:** Show that you can analyze a trade before entry, identify its costs and exposures, complete the execution replay, and record every decision. The four-week Module 12 artifact is an interim review; [Risk Gate 2](RISK_AND_OPERATIONS.md) still requires at least eight weeks and its full decision record before it can pass. Paper profits do not pass either gate by themselves.

## Stage 3 — quantitative foundation (weeks 49–72)

Study [Teaching Volume 3](LESSONS_03_QUANT.md) alongside this map.

No coding experience is assumed. Start statistics with [Khan Academy's Statistics and Probability](https://www.khanacademy.org/math/statistics-probability) and programming with [MIT's introductory Python course](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-100l-introduction-to-cs-and-programming-using-python-fall-2022/), which is designed for little or no programming experience. Learners who already code can use the [lab sequence](labs/README.md) from the beginning and challenge the syntax portion of Module 15. Use the [official Python tutorial](https://docs.python.org/3/tutorial/) later as a reference; it assumes prior programming knowledge. If algebra and functions are new, begin with the [math bridge](FOUNDATION_MATH_BRIDGE.md) and [Khan Academy's algebra basics](https://www.khanacademy.org/mission/algebra-basics) and extend the timeline. [MIT 18.05](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-05-introduction-to-probability-and-statistics-spring-2022/pages/syllabus/) lists multivariable calculus as a prerequisite; use its deeper problems after the needed calculus. [MIT Analytics of Finance](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-450-analytics-of-finance-fall-2010/pages/syllabus/) is a later graduate-level extension, not an entry course. The 104-week schedule is a target, not a reason to skip prerequisites.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 13. Explain why a 90% win rate can still lose money and why a model probability is not a promise. |
| 14. Statistical inference | 53–56 | Sampling error, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, autocorrelation, selection bias, multiple comparisons. | Complete the relevant Khan inference units, critique three claimed trading edges, and work the [dependent-observation and trial-log lab](labs/README.md). | 15. Python for analysis | 57–60 | New programmers begin with MIT introductory Python; experienced programmers demonstrate data validation, tests, plotting, saved inputs, and reproducible calculations. | A clean-run script that loads a price series, computes returns and realized volatility, plots both, and reproduces the hand-checked results. Experienced programmers replace syntax exercises with a tested [lab artifact](labs/README.md); everyone passes the same reproducibility gate. |
| 16. Data quality | 61–64 | Option identifiers, splits/adjustments, missing quotes, stale markets, survivorship, time zones, corporate actions, point-in-time data. | A data dictionary and QA report. | 17. Pricing logic | 65–68 | No arbitrage, put-call parity, binomial replication, American versus European exercise. | Price simple options with a small binomial tree; show the replicating logic and test parity on idealized examples. |
| 18. **Gate C:** Reproduce calculations from raw inputs, state model assumptions, and identify bad data. Study of stochastic calculus is optional until this foundation is firm; the professional extension is [MIT 15.450](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-450-analytics-of-finance-fall-2010/pages/lecture-notes/).

## Stage 4 — research, portfolio risk, and specialization (weeks 73–104)

Study [Teaching Volume 4](LESSONS_04_RESEARCH.md) alongside this map.

The [research lab](RESEARCH_LAB.md) supplies full protocols and templates; the [runnable labs](labs/README.md) supply small synthetic inputs and checks. Research questions must be stated before examining the final test outcomes. An apparently profitable options strategy must survive realistic spreads, commissions, assignment/settlement assumptions, an untouched later period, and a fair benchmark. Published research on [backtest overfitting](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2326253) and [option premia changing within the authors' historical sample](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31833) motivates this discipline.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 19. Volatility surface | 73–76 | Moneyness, tenor, skew, smile, forward volatility, surface dynamics, calendar/butterfly arbitrage checks. | Plot dated surface slices and write five observations and five data/model caveats. Check strike monotonicity/convexity and solve the qualified two-expiry calendar case in Volume 4, including its cash/share ledger and fresh transfer question. |
| 20. Sources of return | 77–80 | Direction, volatility risk premium, insurance demand, event risk, carry, convexity, hedging costs, competition. | A literature memo comparing three explanations for one proposed edge, with at least one reason the edge could disappear. |
| 21. Research protocol | 81–84 | Define a falsifiable hypothesis, population, contract selector, entry/exit, benchmark, costs, risk, and stopping rule. Check data access, fields, license, timestamp resolution, and budget before finalizing the question. | Pre-register one question and comparator using [the research template](RESEARCH_LAB.md); select an honest evidence level—synthetic system validation, published-index analysis, or licensed historical-contract replay—before looking at results. |
| 22. Historical test or declared alternative | 85–88 | Point-in-time replay, quote-side fills, commissions, corporate actions, assignment, position limits, and the limits of available data. | Reproduce the chosen evidence-level project with a trade, simulation, or published-observation record as appropriate, data QA, sensitivity, and independent checks. A synthetic test validates software; published-index history supports an index comparison; contract-level claims require suitable contract quotes. Begin forward shadow logging where the protocol produces live-observable decisions. |
| 23. Robustness | 89–92 | Chronological holdout where genuinely possible, regime splits, sensitivity, and a complete trial log; for contract-level studies, walk-forward checks and trade dependence. | Report all tried variants and failed results. Reserve a final segment before viewing it when feasible; an already examined public index series remains descriptive. A tests unseen synthetic cases; B tests published-series comparisons and methodology sensitivity; C tests the historical contract rule and continues its forward shadow log. |
| 24. Portfolio risk | 93–96 | Aggregate Greeks, correlated moves, gap/IV/liquidity shocks, margin, concentration, and drawdowns on a hypothetical portfolio. | Stress at least eight joint scenarios using stated contract assumptions and a funding ledger. A can use a synthetic fixture; B's separate published-index study cannot supply constituent contract cash obligations, so add a clearly labeled hypothetical portfolio exercise; C also stresses actual study positions and continues forward logging. |
| 25. Specialization | 97–100 | Use the [three goal paths](MASTERY_TRACKS.md) to choose one current project; within it, investigate hedging/overlay, volatility relative value, event options, systematic index strategies, market microstructure, or options on futures. | Produce a critical review and feasible small test or simulation. Explain its needed mathematics, data, technology, capital, and expertise. Continue the appropriate A/B/C evidence record. |
| 26. Independent audit | 101–104 | Reconcile the selected A/B/C evidence record, comparator, costs or index assumptions, exceptions, and risk; present a research defense. | A level-labeled capstone report and reproducible evidence bundle. Include failed ideas and uncertainty. Have another knowledgeable person challenge assumptions before treating any edge as established. |

**Gate D:** The capstone has a complete decision, simulation, or published-observation record appropriate to its data; an explicit treatment of costs and source assumptions; no obvious future-data leakage; a frozen question and later evaluation where the data permit one; a fair comparator; stress or sensitivity results; and a candid statement of uncertainty. Label its evidence level. A synthetic simulation can pass a software/accounting audit but cannot establish a market edge. A published index comparison does not prove its constituent option trades were replicated. A negative or inconclusive result can pass; concealing a failed test cannot.

## Before considering live capital

The course does not automatically graduate anyone to live trading. Gates A–D are completion standards for this optional advanced research path, rather than universal or regulatory prerequisites for every personal investment decision. A separate live-capital decision requires understanding the intended position and its obligations, reading the current ODD, confirming broker permissions and exact product specifications, considering personal finances and an affordable loss limit, and rehearsing assignment/expiry and order handling. **A synthetic level-A capstone shows that a simulator follows its rules; it does not supply evidence that a live strategy has an edge.** Claims about a strategy's edge need suitable real-market evidence, realistic execution assumptions, and forward observations. Simulated fills and small samples cannot prove an edge. Choosing to remain in observation or simulation is a valid outcome. The extended [risk guide](RISK_AND_OPERATIONS.md) describes an illustrative training policy; personal and broker requirements remain separate from course grades.

The 2026 FINRA intraday margin changes have a broker transition period. Verify the rule your broker actually applies rather than relying on an old universal pattern-day-trader summary. See [FINRA's current explanation](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/intraday-margin-requirements).

## Beyond the core: the multi-year mastery loop

Repeat a disciplined cycle: identify a narrow question; read primary research; make a testable forecast; build point-in-time data; compare with a simple alternative; forward test; cap risk; independently audit; and retire ideas when evidence changes. Over several market regimes, measure **net** returns, drawdowns, tail losses, capacity, execution quality, tax/operational drag, and decision quality. Exceptional trading is an evidence claim, not a certificate or a trade count.

Optional advanced study: [MIT's finance mathematics course](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-642-topics-in-mathematics-with-applications-in-finance-fall-2024/), [MIT Analytics of Finance](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-450-analytics-of-finance-fall-2010/pages/syllabus/), and the publisher-verified books in [SOURCES.md](SOURCES.md). Buy books or historical option data only when a specific project needs them.

After the common core, use [the three path standards](MASTERY_TRACKS.md) for personal portfolio decisions, independent trading research, and professional quant preparation. They require different evidence and may take several additional years. Keep one advanced project active long enough to defend it before changing focus.
